# Data Dunkers Junior — answer keys

The activities use **live class data**, so the exact numbers will be different for every event key. These answers focus on the reasoning, vocabulary, and evidence students should include.

A strong answer usually:

- names the measurement or group;
- gives a number from the chart or table;
- explains what the number means;
- says what the data does **not** prove.

---

## 1. Chart interpretation

**Questions**

1. Which distance has the highest average number of shots made? What is the average?
2. Which distance has the lowest average? What is the average?
3. Describe the pattern as the distance increases from 2 ft to 10 ft.
4. Why might the pattern make sense in basketball?
5. What else would you want to know before saying “shorter shots are always easier”?

**Sample answers**

1. “The highest average was at ___ ft. It was ___ out of 5.” Students should read the tallest bar and the matching value.
2. “The lowest average was at ___ ft. It was ___ out of 5.” Students should read the shortest bar.
3. “As the distance increased, the average mostly decreased.” Accept “stayed about the same” if that is what the class data shows, as long as the student cites the bars.
4. “Shorter shots may be easier because the ball travels less distance and the angle is simpler.” Accept other reasonable basketball explanations.
5. “I would want to know how many players took each shot, whether everyone used the same ball and hoop, and whether players practised before the test.”

**Look for**

- Correct use of “average”.
- Values that match the chart.
- A pattern statement rather than a list of unrelated facts.
- Awareness that the chart does not control everything.

## 2. Averages and spread

**Questions**

1. Which measurement did you choose? What are its mean and median?
2. Which value appears most often (the mode)? If there is no mode, explain why.
3. Find the range. What does the range tell you about the group?
4. Is there an outlier? How does it affect the mean?
5. Would you describe this group as “mostly the same” or “quite different”? Use the spread to support your answer.

**Sample answers**

1. “I chose reaction time. The mean was ___ ms and the median was ___ ms.”
2. “The mode was ___.” If no value repeats, “There is no mode because every value appears only once.”
3. “The range was ___ ms. That means the fastest and slowest values were ___ apart.”
4. “___ looked like an outlier because it was much higher/lower than the rest. It pulls the mean up/down.”
5. “I would say quite different, because the range was large and the standard deviation was ___.” Accept “mostly the same” if the range and spread are small, as long as the student cites evidence.

**Look for**

- Correct mean and median from the site.
- The idea that the mean can be affected by outliers.
- A range described as a difference, not just “big” or “small”.
- A conclusion that matches the spread.

## 3. Scatterplots and relationships

**Questions**

1. Which two measurements did you plot? Describe the pattern in one sentence.
2. What is the value of r? Is the relationship strong, moderate, or weak?
3. Find one player whose dot is far from the trend line. What might explain it?
4. Does the pattern prove that one measurement causes the other? Explain why or why not.
5. If you could collect one more piece of data to test the relationship, what would you collect and why?

**Sample answers**

1. “I plotted height and wingspan. Players with greater height tended to have greater wingspan.”
2. “r = ___. That is a strong/moderate/weak positive/negative relationship.” Use the site’s r value.
3. “___ was far above the line. Maybe they have a different training history, or the measurement was taken differently.”
4. “No. The pattern shows they move together, but it does not prove that one causes the other. A third factor, like age or overall body size, could affect both.”
5. “I would collect age or training hours, because those could explain both height and wingspan.”

**Look for**

- Direction words: positive, negative, no clear pattern.
- Strength words: strong, moderate, weak.
- A specific outlier.
- A clear statement that correlation is not causation.
- A plausible third variable.

## 4. Shots and percentages

**Questions**

1. Which distance has the highest shooting percentage? Which has the lowest? Give the percentages.
2. How many attempts are behind each percentage? Why does the number of attempts matter?
3. A player makes 3 out of 5 shots at 10 ft and 1 out of 5 at 2 ft. What can you say about this player? What can you not say?
4. What is the class free-throw percentage? How does it compare with the shot percentages?
5. Which distance would you recommend practising most? Use evidence from the table.

**Sample answers**

1. “The highest was ___ ft at ___%. The lowest was ___ ft at ___%.”
2. “Each distance has 5 attempts per player. The table shows ___ players, so there were ___ attempts. More attempts make the percentage a more reliable estimate.”
3. “In this sample, the player did better at 10 ft than at 2 ft. I cannot say that 10 ft is always easier for them, because this was only five shots at each distance.”
4. “The class free-throw percentage was ___%. It was higher/lower than most of the shot percentages.” Accept a comparison supported by the numbers.
5. “I would practise ___ ft because it had the lowest percentage, ___%, across ___ attempts.”

**Look for**

- Correct percentage calculation: made ÷ attempted × 100.
- Attention to the number of attempts.
- Recognition that a small sample can be misleading.
- A recommendation tied to evidence.

## 5. Probability and predictions

**Questions**

1. Which distance has the highest probability of a made shot? Which has the lowest?
2. For your chosen distance and number of attempts, what is the expected number of makes? Show the multiplication.
3. Run the simulation. Did the average match the expected value? Why might it be a little different?
4. If you simulated 10,000 players instead of 1,000, would you expect the average to get closer to the expected value or farther away? Explain.
5. Why is a probability from a small sample less trustworthy than one from a large sample?

**Sample answers**

1. “The highest probability was at ___ ft, about ___%. The lowest was at ___ ft, about ___%.”
2. “The probability was ___ (or ___%). Expected makes = ___ × ___ = ___.”
3. “The simulation average was close to but not exactly the expected value. Random results vary from trial to trial.”
4. “Closer. More trials usually give an average closer to the expected value because random ups and downs cancel out.”
5. “A small sample can be affected by luck. A large sample gives a more stable estimate.”

**Look for**

- Probability written as a decimal or percent.
- Correct expected-value multiplication.
- “Expected” not treated as “guaranteed”.
- The law-of-large-numbers idea in student-friendly language.

## 6. Misleading graphs

**Questions**

1. Look at Version A and Version B. Describe one difference between them.
2. Why does Version B make the differences look larger? Use the word “axis” in your answer.
3. Which version is fairer for comparing the distances? Explain your reasoning.
4. Version C shows only two distances. What might a reader wrongly conclude if they did not see the full chart?
5. Write a fair chart title for the shot data. Make sure it does not overclaim.

**Sample answers**

1. “Version A starts its vertical axis at 0, while Version B starts closer to the smallest value.”
2. “Version B uses a truncated axis, so a small change takes up a lot of vertical space and looks bigger.”
3. “Version A is fairer because it shows the full scale from zero and does not exaggerate the differences.”
4. “A reader might think the two shown distances are the only ones, or that the gap is the whole story. They might miss the other distances and the sample size.”
5. “Average shots made by distance for our class” or “Class shooting averages at five distances”. The title should not say “best” or “worst” unless the data supports it.

**Look for**

- Identification of the axis range.
- The word “truncated” or a clear explanation of starting above zero.
- Recognition of cherry-picking.
- A fair, specific title.

## 7. Data storytelling

**Questions**

1. Write a headline for your data story. Keep it short and fair.
2. What is your claim? Which number from the chart or table supports it?
3. Why should a classmate or coach care about this pattern?
4. What is one limitation of your evidence?
5. If you could collect one more measurement to make the story stronger, what would it be?

**Sample answers**

1. “Our class made the most shots from 2 ft” or “Reaction times varied widely in our class”. The headline should match the chart.
2. “My claim is that the class average was highest at ___ ft. The chart shows ___ out of 5, compared with ___ at ___ ft.”
3. “A coach could use this to decide which distance to practise, or to see where players need more support.”
4. “The sample is small, not everyone completed every distance, and we only tested once.”
5. “I would collect more attempts per player, or record whether players had practised before the test.”

**Look for**

- A claim that the chart can actually support.
- A specific number as evidence.
- A “so what” that connects to a real audience.
- An honest limitation.
- A next step that would improve the evidence.

## General look-fors across all activities

| Skill | What to notice |
| --- | --- |
| Reading charts | Uses the title, axes, labels, and units. |
| Using evidence | Quotes a number and says what it means. |
| Comparing groups | Mentions sample size and fairness. |
| Explaining | Goes beyond “because it is” to a mechanism or reason. |
| Critical thinking | Names a limitation or an alternative explanation. |
| Communication | Writes clearly for a classmate, not just the teacher. |

## A note about “wrong” answers

Many activity questions ask for an interpretation, not a single fact. A response can be different from the samples and still be strong if it is supported by the class data. Look for evidence and reasoning rather than exact wording.
